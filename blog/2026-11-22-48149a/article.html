OpenAI Launches Astra at Four Times Sol's API Price

On November 17, OpenAI introduced GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol. Astra is intended for demanding professional work, research, and complex software development. Sol is the faster, lower-priced option for everyday multistep tasks. The OpenAI API offers both models. ChatGPT Work and Codex desktop offer them in preview, with access in managed workspaces subject to seat and administrator settings.

The pricing gap gives teams a reason to route work deliberately. At standard API rates, Astra costs $8 per million input tokens, $0.40 per million cached input tokens, and $40 per million output tokens. Sol costs $2, $0.10, and $10 respectively. Each Astra rate is four times Sol’s. A difficult assignment that requires several Sol attempts and extensive review could pay back the added model charge. An easy assignment both models complete on the first try leaves the price gap on the bill.

Take an illustrative task with 10,000 uncached input tokens and 2,000 billable output tokens, including reasoning. At these rates, Sol costs $0.04 and Astra $0.16, a $0.12 difference. Suppose an escalation takes five minutes of a reviewer’s time at $60 an hour, or $5. With equal token use and all else held constant, Astra would offset its extra model charge if it reduced the escalation rate by more than 2.4 percentage points. The break-even rate follows from those assumed token counts and labor costs.

OpenAI says the newer models use fewer output tokens on demanding work and reports improvements in professional documents, coding, and computer use. Those are provider claims drawn from particular evaluations. An individual team should test its own documents, systems, and definitions of success before treating a benchmark as a savings figure. A polished report with a missing source or an untested code change may still require expensive human repair.

Start with comparable jobs and blind review where practical. Record model charges, retries, time to a usable first draft, review and correction time, and errors found after acceptance. On a document, a useful measure could be the cost of a cited answer that a reviewer approves. On code, it could be the cost of a tested change that meets the ticket's requirements. Keep task difficulty and acceptance criteria stable so that the comparison measures performance on the same work.

The boundary around data and authority matters as much as model choice. A model able to browse or operate tools should receive only the access needed for the task, with human approval for consequential actions. A better answer does not by itself authorize a payment, disclosure, or production deployment. Nor should confidential material be sent to a workflow before its access, retention, and organizational controls are understood.

Over the next two to three quarters, a team can begin with Sol on low-risk work and compare Astra on a representative sample, including difficult cases. Escalation makes sense when the reduction in failed attempts or reviewer time exceeds the additional model and operating cost at a consistent quality standard. If total cost rises without better acceptance or fewer downstream corrections, keep the simpler route. The conclusion belongs to the buyer's measured workflow, not to a model name.

Microsoft Launches Cobalt 200 Cloud VMs on Azure

Microsoft announced general availability of Azure Cobalt 200 virtual machines at Ignite on November 17, after an earlier preview. The second-generation, 3-nanometer Arm-based chip has 132 Neoverse V3 cores, and VM sizes go up to 128 vCPUs. They target general-purpose workloads, including the database, search, and other CPU work surrounding AI agents. Microsoft says Cobalt 200 delivers up to 50% more CPU performance than the preceding generation. Microsoft reported that ceiling for selected workloads. Application benchmarks on comparable configurations would show what an individual customer could gain.

Azure lists up to 85 Gbps of network bandwidth and up to 70 Gbps of remote-storage bandwidth for eligible configurations. Those limits apply to different paths. Application throughput also depends on protocol overhead and the size and concurrency of requests.

A production agent reads data, searches indexes, calls services, runs application code, and records results around each model call. If those surrounding steps become faster or require fewer resources, the cost and latency of the whole workflow can improve even without changing the model. A slow external API can still dominate the end-to-end response time.

Migration has its own bill. Teams moving software from x86 to Arm must check that operating systems, packages, native libraries, container images, security tools, and monitoring agents work correctly. They also need realistic performance tests for memory, storage, networking, and load spikes. Extra engineering time or a broken dependency can absorb the first year of compute savings.

Price per virtual machine is only the starting point. Compare completed requests at a fixed quality and response-time target, including long-tail latency, peak capacity, idle resources, and support effort. Higher machine density could reduce the bill if it holds under peak traffic. A customer's negotiated credits and reservations determine what they pay. Microsoft carries its own design and operating costs.

For the cloud provider, custom silicon ties spending on design, capacity, and data centers to utilization over time. The financial result depends on utilization and the cost of fabrication and depreciation. For a customer, the test is whether a comparable service meets its reliability target at a lower cost per accepted request after migration.

GlobalFoundries Plans to Expand Spot Patrols Across Four Fabs

On November 17, Boston Dynamics and GlobalFoundries announced an expansion of their Spot inspection program across four semiconductor fabrication sites in New York, Vermont, Germany, and Singapore. GlobalFoundries says two Spot robots in Singapore have traveled nearly 900 kilometers and completed more than 90,000 inspections in sub-fab areas since April, with a 99% mission success rate. Those are company-reported operating figures for that deployment.

The figures show repeated field work on an existing deployment. The announcement gives a mission success rate and leaves out the mission count, its success criteria, and how often a technician had to step in. The maintenance team still needs figures on detected faults, false alarms, and interruptions. A robot can complete its route while a sensor misses a developing fault. Conversely, a flagged condition can still require a person to diagnose and fix it.

The economic comparison should include the entire route from observation to repair. A facility needs the robots, sensors, network, maintenance, software, staff training, and an escalation process when something looks wrong. It should also count human time spent responding to false alerts and the time needed when a robot cannot complete a route. The relevant benefit is improved reliability or lower cost while preserving inspection coverage.

The companies also plan to bring Boston Dynamics' humanoid Atlas onsite in early 2027. The onsite trial will test which tasks Atlas can perform and how much human intervention they require. Spot's inspection record offers a separate operating baseline. Before either becomes an assumed productivity gain across the factory, look for a defined task, measured intervention rate, safety performance, and the cost of maintaining the system in daily use.

How to Measure the Cost of Work That Passes Review

These announcements move at different speeds. OpenAI has published model and API prices. Microsoft has made a processor-based cloud service generally available. GlobalFoundries has disclosed an existing inspection record and plans to expand it. Customers still have to measure how those changes affect accepted work, operating cost, and time to resolve problems.

Through the first half of 2027, model buyers can compare total spending and reviewer time per accepted task, together with corrections discovered later. Cloud customers can compare total cost per completed request at the same reliability and response-time targets after migration. Manufacturers can compare inspection coverage, unresolved alerts, human intervention, and maintenance cost alongside any change in downtime. Each test needs a baseline and a consistent definition of success.

There are several possible results. Lower prices or faster machines may translate into cheaper accepted work. Teams might instead use the savings on more ambitious tasks, leaving total spending unchanged while improving output. Or rework, integration, and supervision may absorb the quoted savings. None of those outcomes can be inferred from a price table, a processor benchmark, or a robot's mission count alone.

The strongest case for expanding a pilot is sustained improvement across cost, quality, and operating risk. If one improves while another deteriorates, the trade-off needs to be explicit. A baseline makes it possible to check the decision again next quarter.
