U.S. Prices Slowed, but Factory Costs Stayed High

Inflation slowed in the U.S., China's exports surged, and oil fell again last week. That sounds like relief. But manufacturers are still paying for inputs, shipping insurance still costs money, and a Hong Kong developer needs fresh cash from shareholders.

These six stories are worth a closer look. A headline price can change while the bill stays with someone else. The answer depends on who has pricing power, who needs financing and who is stuck with the risk.

U.S. CPI and PPI tell different parts of the cost story. On Nov 10, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 0.4% in October. That was slower than September's 0.6% increase. Food prices rose 0.5%, while shelter costs increased 0.3%. Then, on Nov 13, the producer price index showed a different pressure point. Final demand prices rose 0.3% for the month. Their year-over-year increase reached 6.5%, the fastest annual pace since November 2022. The two indexes measure different transactions and cannot be directly subtracted.

A slower monthly CPI increase does not mean that prices are falling. Consumers are still paying more, just at a slower pace. The tougher question is where the producer's bill ends up. A company with a strong brand or control over distribution may raise its selling prices. A business facing intense competition may have to accept a smaller profit margin instead. That financial strain might show up in earnings before it appears in consumer inflation.

None of this means that a 6.5% increase in producer prices will become a 6.5% increase in consumer prices. Retailers and manufacturers will show the answer in their earnings. Check input costs, selling prices and gross margins. If customers resist higher prices while suppliers keep charging more, the businesses in the middle may be squeezed first. Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Nov 10 and thirteenth.

China's trade surplus is not the same thing as factory profit. Trade figures cited in the IMF's Nov 10 market monitoring report showed a sharp rise in China's October exports. Exports measured in U.S. dollars increased 35.2% from a year earlier, while imports rose 41.6%. The monthly trade surplus was about US$105.3 billion. The accumulated surplus for the first ten months topped US$1 trillion, and high-tech exports climbed 66.6%. Meanwhile, China's statistics agency reported October consumer inflation of 0.7% and producer price inflation of 5.7%.

Exports can set records while factories struggle to make money. Sales are not profit, and the trade surplus is not cash that companies can spend. Higher imports can reflect expensive energy, industrial parts or technology purchases. They do not necessarily prove that households are spending much more. When raw materials cost more, factories need enough pricing power to pass that increase to overseas buyers. If customers have alternatives, suppliers may have to absorb the extra expense.

Overseas customers keep Chinese production lines busy. They also make those factories exposed to global investment cycles. Demand for high-tech equipment can provide valuable growth. But orders linked to global artificial intelligence spending may also weaken if that investment cycle slows. To test whether the trade boom benefits manufacturers, follow industrial profits, export volumes and prices, and the conversion of orders into operating cash. A record surplus alone cannot answer that question. Sources: IMF market monitoring, Nov 10; National Bureau of Statistics of China, Nov 9.

1 · ENERGY

Oil, Shipping and the Cost of a Weaker Yen

Cheaper oil does not immediately mean cheaper transport. On Nov 13, Brent crude futures finished at US$95.84 a barrel and U.S. West Texas Intermediate at US$88.56. Both benchmarks fell by roughly 2.5% over the week. Reuters reported that traders were increasingly focused on the possibility of excess global supply. Softer U.S. petroleum demand also weighed on the market. At the same time, security concerns around Middle Eastern shipping and the Strait of Hormuz remained unresolved.

Oil is cheaper on the futures screen. That does not mean airlines, refiners or trucking firms get an equally large discount right away. Importers may benefit first. Refiners, airlines and logistics firms still face existing contracts, inventory costs, freight charges and insurance premiums. Their hedging arrangements also matter. And a 2.5% weekly decline does not make oil cheap when Brent is still above US$95 a barrel.

The futures market is betting on more supply. The cost of moving actual barrels may tell a different story. Expectations of a surplus can push futures prices down while shipping risks keep transport costs high. That difference matters to the people who actually buy and move fuel. Watch U.S. Energy Information Administration inventories, actual shipments, shipping insurance rates and refinery margins. If those costs remain elevated, lower oil futures may not translate into equally lower operating costs. Source: Reuters energy report, Nov 13.

The U.S. and Japan bought yen in July, but the disclosure came in November. On Nov 12, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York disclosed third-quarter foreign exchange operations. The report showed that the New York Fed, acting for the U.S. Treasury, bought ¥79.4 billion on July 31. The transaction was worth roughly US$497 million. Japanese authorities also intervened heavily during the quarter. The timing matters: the trade happened in July. November's news was the release of the official record, not a new intervention that week.

Government purchases can move a currency sharply in the short term. But they do not automatically change the longer-term flows that determine its value. A weaker yen may help Japanese exporters earn more in local currency. It can also raise the cost of imported fuel and other essentials. That shifts the burden toward importers and households. The U.S. Treasury's decision to act through the New York Fed should not be confused with a separate monetary policy decision by the Federal Reserve.

Reports described the dollar moving from roughly one hundred and sixty three yen toward one hundred and fifty five during the intervention window, before the yen softened again. A day's intervention can move the exchange rate without changing the trade and investment flows behind it. Official buying can interrupt a trend without permanently reversing interest-rate differences, trade needs or investor positioning. Judge its effectiveness by comparing the actual trade date with later exchange rates, interest differentials and import bills. Sources: New York Fed third-quarter report, released Nov 12; RTHK, Nov 13.

2 · LIQUIDITY

Who Funds the Debt and Who Gets Paid

New World Development is asking shareholders to fund more time. On Nov 15, Hong Kong's New World Development proposed a rights issue to raise about HK$24.9 billion. The proposal offered eleven new shares for every existing share, at HK$0.90 each. That was well below the stock's previous closing price of HK$4.87. The company planned to use part of the proceeds to repay ten per cent of roughly HK$82 billion in bank loans early. It also sought to extend the maturity of most of the remaining debt by two years. These were proposed terms, not completed financing.

For a small shareholder, the cash demand is hard to miss. Someone holding one old share would be entitled to buy eleven new shares. Taking up that entitlement would cost HK$9.90. That is more than twice the old share's quoted value before the announcement. A shareholder who does not participate, or otherwise manage the rights, could face substantial dilution. However, dilution must be assessed alongside the value of the rights and the adjusted share price. It is not correct to treat the difference between quoted prices as a direct cash loss.

Banks stand to receive some repayment and a revised debt timetable. Shareholders face a decision about putting fresh money into a company with difficult property assets. Rights financing can buy time, but it cannot make buildings easier to sell or eliminate project risks. Property takes time to sell. A bank loan comes due on the day written in the contract. Follow the final rights terms, participation rate, net debt and actual property-sale proceeds. Sources: New World Development's Nov 15 proposal and RTHK reporting.

Mitsui Chemicals is buying specialized chip materials with substantial borrowed money. On Nov 9, Japan's Mitsui Chemicals announced plans to acquire semiconductor materials company JSR. Its transaction materials indicated an equity valuation of roughly ¥1.3921 trillion. Adding about ¥447.9 billion in interest-bearing debt produced an enterprise value near ¥1.84 trillion. That was around sixteen times expected EBITDA for fiscal 2026. The Development Bank of Japan planned to contribute two hundred billion yen, mainly through non-voting preferred shares. The transaction was expected to close in the second half of fiscal 2027, subject to conditions.

Advanced chip materials, including photoresists, need years of research and customer qualification. Those barriers can make suppliers valuable as artificial intelligence demand grows. But the price tag is only the first part of the buyer's bill. Mitsui Chemicals planned about ¥1.64 trillion in bridge financing, followed by refinancing. It also set a goal of reducing leverage within three years. Consider the financing cost. One extra percentage point of annual interest on that bridge loan would add roughly ¥16.4 billion a year. This is a sensitivity example, not Mitsui's reported interest bill.

The seller has an exit. The lender backs an industrial deal. Mitsui's shareholders carry the financing and integration risks. The seller secures an exit, while the public-sector lender supports an industrial combination. The buyer hopes to capture specialized materials earnings, but its shareholders take on financing and integration risk. The test is whether customer retention, margins and cash generation can support the purchase price. Watch the final financing terms, debt reduction and operating results rather than assuming that a strong technology franchise guarantees a good acquisition. Sources: Mitsui Chemicals' Nov 9 presentation and JSR's announcement.

3 · FOCUS

Follow the Cash, Not Just the Headline

U.S. retailers may be absorbing costs that shoppers refuse to pay. Chinese factories may be winning orders without much more profit per order. Falling oil futures do not remove the price of insurance and delivery. Governments can buy currency, but they cannot instantly rewrite the flows behind an exchange rate. Banks can extend debt, while shareholders may have to provide the cash. A company can acquire valuable technology and still overpay for financing.

When the next results arrive, start with the accounts. Margins and cash flow can tell a different story from export growth and the price of oil. Over the coming week, look for updates in company margins, export prices, energy inventories, freight and insurance costs, the New World rights offering, Mitsui's financing and yen market flows. If those measurements contradict the headline story, the explanation needs to change.
