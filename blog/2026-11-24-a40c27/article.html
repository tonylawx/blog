Chips are taking the hit, but the broader market is still holding up

U.S. stocks split on November 24. The S&P 500 closed at 7,201.09, down 0.50%. The Nasdaq fell 1.48%. The Dow gained 0.85%, and the Russell 2000 added 0.57%. Breadth across the S&P 500 remained positive, with 348 advancers and 154 decliners. Most stocks outside technology were still higher. Weakness was concentrated in large-cap tech, with semiconductors under the heaviest pressure.

The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 7.88%, with all 30 constituents lower. It had surged 14.03% in the prior session, then gave back 7.88% the next day. Over the two sessions combined, the index was still up about 5.04%. The key point is not that the entire rebound disappeared. It is that buyers did not provide much follow-through after the sharp rally. Nvidia fell 5.86%, AMD dropped 8.86%, Broadcom lost 3.98%, and Micron slid 11.01%. The broader tape did not show market-wide panic, but chip investors were clearly reluctant to chase the bounce.

Tariff headlines added another layer of uncertainty. The U.S. Trade Representative was nearing the release of findings from an industrial-capacity investigation covering 16 major trading partners. New import tariffs were possible, but the rates were still unknown. At the same time, consumer confidence rose 11.4 points to 103.3 in November, above the 92.5 consensus. Better consumer and labour-market expectations do not fit a picture of an economy suddenly falling apart. The market's weakness was more concentrated than that.

1 · EARNINGS

The AI buildout has a timing gap between revenue and risk

Revenue recognition and risk absorption happen at different points in the AI supply chain.

Chip and networking suppliers can book orders and sales once equipment is delivered. Data-centre operators have to fund land, power, facilities, servers, and networking up front. That funding can come from operating cash flow, bonds, loans, leases, equity, or customer prepayments. Once the capacity is online, operators still carry utilization, depreciation, interest, and maintenance costs. The final test is whether enterprise customers and end users keep paying for training, inference, and software services.

That is why rising AI capital spending and attractive investment returns are two different things. Hardware orders can turn into reported revenue early. Data-centre utilization and recurring customer payments take longer to prove. When construction runs ahead of demand, the first pressure may show up at operators with heavy debt, frequent refinancing needs, and concentrated customers. It does not have to hit every chip supplier equally.

Credit markets are starting to examine that chain. Public five-year credit default swap prints show Oracle around 368 basis points and CoreWeave around 1,096. Nvidia was around 143 basis points and Broadcom around 183. Meta was near 114, while Amazon was around 93. The latest trade dates are not identical across issuers. Some single-name trading is also thin. These are public transaction prints, not firm dealer marks or executable quotes.

2 · FOCUS

Wider CDS spreads are not a default verdict

A wider credit default swap spread means investors are demanding more money for credit protection. It reflects a higher risk premium and can signal more expensive funding. It does not mean a company has defaulted, and it cannot be translated directly into a certain default probability. A rating downgrade needs to be checked against a formal rating-agency notice, the rated entity, and the specific debt instrument. Labels in a market chart are not enough.

The important divide is between companies that can fund investment internally and those that need outside capital to keep expanding. Cash-rich cloud platforms can slow spending or change project priorities. Compute operators that rely on rolling debt and higher-cost funding face more direct pressure. Even when companies share the same view on AI demand, their funding costs determine how quickly they can grow and how much stress their balance sheets can absorb.

Investors are also watching circular financing and off-balance-sheet commitments. A market concern is not the same as a confirmed transaction structure. Complicated financing does not make every order fake. But when customers, suppliers, and lenders depend on one another, a slowdown in end-customer payments can travel through contracts and balance sheets.

3 · SEMIS

Multiple compression comes first; earnings cuts would be the next test

The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index hit an intraday high of 11,731.31 on October 28. It closed at 8,832.50 on November 24, about 24.7% below that high. That puts the index in technical bear-market territory. It describes the price drawdown, not the collapse of the AI thesis. Share prices can compress valuation multiples before earnings expectations are revised lower.

There are two separate risks to track. The first is multiple compression: investors are willing to pay less for the same expected earnings. The second is an EPS decline: orders, revenue, margins, or cash flow start coming in below prior expectations. The first can happen without earnings cuts. The second puts both earnings and valuation under pressure.

Memory stocks run on a different cycle. Micron fell more than 11% on the day, but memory pricing, inventories, and supply-demand conditions can change quickly. A low forward P/E does not automatically mean a memory stock is cheap. If memory prices fall, earnings per share can turn lower before the valuation looks expensive. For the broader semiconductor group, the next test is whether earnings estimates for Nvidia, Micron, and Broadcom are cut together, not just whether the index has another bad session.

Bring the single names back to their price zones

AMD closed at $413.27 on November 24, with an intraday low of $396.01. It also closed below its roughly $432 200-day moving average. The $400 to $424 area is the first zone it needs to reclaim. If closes remain below that band, the next historical volume area worth watching sits below $366. That is not a guaranteed downside target. The more useful signal is a quick reclaim after a breakdown, followed by buyers defending the retest.

TSM closed at $349.20. After the gap higher on November 23, it traded as low as $339.73 the following day, moving below the prior gap band from $343.75 to $361.66. It then closed back inside that range. The gap fill weakens the rebound setup. The next test is whether the stock can reclaim $361.66 and attract a second wave of buyers on a pullback. ASML closed at $1,336.06 and showed a similar structure. Around $1,200 is a nearer watch level, but the stronger support reference remains the $1,000 to $1,090 area.

Broadcom closed at $301.08, with an intraday low of $297.80. That undercut its November 20 low of $301.02, and the close finished near that level. A lower wick near a fresh low does not prove buyers have taken control. Marvell closed at $131.57 after touching $128.14. The $103 to $125 area is a lower historical trading zone to monitor. It only becomes meaningful support if a retest produces a long lower wick and follow-through buying.

Nvidia closed at $155.38, with a low of $150.50. It remains just above the $132 to $150 support zone. Resistance sits between $174 and $194. A bounce after a weak open is still only a repair attempt if volume does not improve and price cannot reclaim key levels. It is too early to call that a new trend.

5 · FOCUS

The S&P 500 is sitting near 7,200

The S&P 500 closed at 7,201.09, almost exactly on 7,200. That is the near-term line to watch. The 7,100 area is the more important downside stress test. If the index holds 7,200 and forms a second leg of strong buying near the prior lows, staged entries become more reasonable. A break below 7,100 would make the short-term structure materially weaker. These are observation levels, not guaranteed support.

There is little reason to rush orders in the first 30 minutes after the open. The final hour matters more, especially the last 30 minutes, when buyers have to show whether they are willing to absorb supply. A rebound without follow-through is not the same as a trend reversal.

The real test is not whether AI demand disappears in one day. It is whether the spending can turn into cash returns. If data-centre utilization, cloud revenue, and customer payments keep growing while operating cash flow covers funding costs, the current move looks more like a volatile valuation reset. If funding costs keep rising, utilization disappoints, capital spending slows, and earnings estimates for Nvidia, Micron, and Broadcom are cut together, the risk moves from valuations to fundamentals. The semiconductor index is already in a technical bear market. Whether the underlying business thesis has broken still depends on those operating and cash-flow measures.
