Google launches Gemini 4 Hydrogen at $1.50 per million API input tokens and opens Search access

On November 10, Google said in an official blog post that Gemini 4 Carbon was open to paying developers and enterprise customers. The introductory price is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. A footnote says that introductory price ends on November 30, after which the rate becomes $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens. The same day, the frontier model reached the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search only for Google AI Ultra subscribers. On November 12 the same author posted again and introduced Gemini 4 Hydrogen at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens, flat across a one-million-token context window.

The price only means something next to the door it walks through. The Hydrogen post says Search is available to all users, and that free users meet the model when automatic selection picks it. Google describes Hydrogen as the tier teams move to from Gemini 3.8 Flash, built for fast execution, while Carbon and Argon stay on the longer problems. One million input tokens cost $1.50 on Hydrogen, $2 on Carbon during the introduction, and $4 after November 30. Output is $7.50 against $10, and $20 after the introduction ends. What fell is the price of a call. Training spend already on the books does not stop depreciating because Search changed its default.

Upstream chip sellers get paid by the cluster. Downstream model sellers get paid by the token. GOOGL put the cheaper execution model into Search and used its own traffic, so the bill does not pass through another cloud. Labs still selling an API by the call now face a $1.50 tier sitting in the search box. Enterprise buying will not stop at a leaderboard rank. Permissions, where the data sits, and who reviews a bad answer stay in the contract. The cut hits the premium first. Integration, evaluation, and human review stay where they were.

Set this next to the cloud price wars that opened the last decade, when compute and storage list prices were cut round after round and the margin that remained often sat in data that could no longer be moved. The rhyme is that a metered door moved down another tier. The difference is distribution. The door is the search box, and anyone who opens a browser is already standing on the price list. Fiber laid in the ground in 1999 still needed a last mile before a user could be billed. A default model in Search does not. A call can start the day the post goes up.

The next two quarters should be checked against the price list, not a benchmark table. If Hydrogen's input price leaves $1.50 before the end of the first quarter of 2027, or if Search stops offering that tier to every user, the claim that a cheap model became the default door has to be rewritten. Whether Carbon actually moves from $2 to $4 on November 30 is the next line on the same sheet. Carbon's Search door that day was Ultra-only. Hydrogen's post says all users. Ranks in the company blog do not replace either price change, or that difference in who can walk in.

SpaceX plans over $74 billion in computing campuses with on-site power in Texas

On November 12, Reuters and the Wall Street Journal both reported that SpaceX plans two supercomputing campuses, in Texas and Arizona, at more than $74 billion combined. The Rio Grande Valley project near Starbase is about $24 billion. The company said it will generate its own power and will not draw it from the public grid. Bloomberg wrote the same day that the Texas site includes a gas-fired plant. The Journal's figures are about $24 billion in Texas and about $50 billion in Cochise County, Arizona. Reuters said the Arizona project still needs county approval.

$74 billion is a plan, not cash already spent. Reuters put the jobs at about 5,500 construction roles and 1,000 permanent roles in Arizona, and more than 500 permanent roles in Texas once the site is running. The Starbase City Commission is due to consider a property-tax abatement on December 9. County approval, the plant, and the tax break are all still in process. What was written down on announcement day is an investment intention and a site, not load already on a grid. The bars below are only the two amounts two news wires have both described. They are not finished capacity.

The same week a model price moved down by the token, another company wrote a power plant into its own fence at a scale of tens of billions of dollars. The Texas project says it will not use the public grid, so the user of the power and the generator sit inside one project instead of on a utility bill. Arizona still needs local approval. Residents, the county, and the company are still at the same permit table. For the last few years, cloud buyers have waited in interconnect queues. This plan puts generation inside the wall. Whether margin stays depends on whether power is an owned cost or a purchased operating bill.

Set that next to 1999, when North American telecom laid backbone fiber ahead of paying users. What was missing then was the on-ramp, not the cable. What this buildout is short of, more often, is power and permission, not the dollar figure in the release. A gas plant on site moves the wait from a grid queue to equipment delivery and emissions permits. Bloomberg's plant, if it is really inside the fence, means the substation line is no longer the first door. The constraint changed tables. It did not disappear. Racks can be ordered by the quarter. Plants, county hearings, and the December 9 tax vote run on another calendar.

The next two or three quarters only have to watch three things already in the reporting. Whether Cochise County approves the site. Whether the December 9 tax abatement passes. Whether Texas still says the power is self-generated, rather than quietly connecting to the public grid. If two of the three fail, more than $74 billion is still a Thursday statement. Reuters also reported that AI was already more than half of the company's third-quarter revenue. That is a mix already on the books. It does not replace a permit.

Microsoft 365 outage lasts over 24 hours while OpenAI unveils user-approved Wallet payments

Around midday in the United States on November 12, Microsoft 365 started failing across a wide area. The Associated Press and Reuters both reported that Outlook, Teams, and Copilot were hit, and that the company did not say service was fully restored until Saturday, November 14. Reuters called the outage more than 24 hours. Microsoft told the Associated Press the cause was an earlier network-capacity upgrade, that it was not a cyber incident, and that it was not caused by AI agents. The Associated Press, citing the company's own site, said more than 430 million people use Microsoft 365. Outage reports came from more than 70 countries and territories. Government offices in Japan, Singapore, and Australia said publicly that they were affected.

Mail, meetings, and the assistant stopped inside one suite. Reuters reported that Euronext, the exchange operator, told listed companies to use the phone and to verify who was on the line. The same report said some airlines warned customers that communication would lag. Most institutions said the core trading systems were still running. What slowed was the talk around those systems. One company's network upgrade became the external line for agencies and airlines in several countries. Central buying is convenient on a normal day. From Thursday to Saturday it was one failure domain. Copilot going down with the inbox also means the assistant had no separate way out.

On November 13, OpenAI published ChatGPT Wallet on its own site, with American Express, Mastercard, and Visa named as partners. The agent does not receive the full card number. The user reviews the merchant, the items, the total, and the selected card, then approves with a face scan or a fingerprint. The terms also let a user grant, in advance, the authority to approve each purchase. The wallet holds no balance and extends no credit. The first rollout is only for ChatGPT Pro users in the United States, aged 18 or older, using a card issued in the United States. The privacy notice is effective November 12. The product post is dated the next day.

The outage and the wallet landed in the same week, and they are not the same fact. Companies have already handed calendars and mail to a few vendors, and the fallback when those stop is the telephone. A consumer agent that wants to spend money still needs a person to nod, and only inside one country and one subscription tier. There is no public count of completed purchases, so the unit economics of agent checkout are not on the page yet. What can be checked now is whether approval stays with the user, and whether the card networks will keep the real number outside the agent by using a one-time credential.

The next quarter has two documents that can force a rewrite. If Microsoft's final incident note puts the outage under 24 hours, or moves the cause off the network-capacity upgrade, the outage paragraph changes. If Wallet is still only U.S. Pro at the end of the first quarter of 2027, and no transaction volume is disclosed, agent payments are still a narrow trial. If the advance-authorization clause becomes the default, the per-purchase nod is no longer the rule written today. The entrance to the card networks has not moved because a wallet post went up.

Next two quarters will test Gemini prices, campus permits, Microsoft's outage and Wallet adoption

This week's technology news does not fit inside one personnel story. On November 10 Google opened a frontier model to paying developers at an introductory $2 per million input tokens. On November 12 an execution model at $1.50 per million input tokens reached Search for every user. The same day SpaceX announced more than $74 billion of campus plans, with Texas power kept inside the project. From Thursday into Saturday, Microsoft 365 was down for more than 24 hours. On Friday OpenAI let an agent pay with a one-time credential after a U.S. Pro user approved the charge.

The financing calendar was the same week, and it does not explain the three items above. On November 10 Reuters reported that Anthropic plans to sell 825 million shares at $70 to $78, raising up to $64.4 billion, with a fully diluted value of about $1.4 trillion at the top of the range, plus a $26 billion private placement of non-voting shares. Run-rate revenue as of October 31 was $81 billion, Reuters wrote. Trading on Nasdaq is expected on November 19. That is a lab raising money. It is not why Search cut a price or why a Texas plant was put inside a fence.

Read as four lines, the directions are separate. The price of a call is moving down, and Search made the cheap tier a default door. Compute spending is moving up, and one buyer has started writing the power plant into the project boundary. The office-suite failure shows that a single point in a work process reaches more people in a day than a model leaderboard does. Agent payments are still stuck at a per-purchase approval and a single subscription tier. A lab whose run-rate is $81 billion still has to face an offering range next week. That is a different book.

The next two quarters need four checks. Whether Hydrogen's $1.50 input price is changed. Whether Cochise County approves the site and whether the December 9 tax abatement passes. Whether Microsoft's incident note still says a network-capacity upgrade and more than 24 hours. Whether Wallet leaves U.S. Pro, and whether Anthropic's offering price leaves the $70 to $78 range. If two of the four fail, the reading above has to change. There is no need to wait for the next leaderboard.

The public line is short. Models got cheaper, power and permits got more expensive, and people remembered the telephone when the office suite stopped. The $1.50 price in Search has to survive the sheet after November 30. The $50 billion Arizona campus has to survive the county. The wallet has to show that someone actually uses the per-purchase approval. The next dates on the page are November 30 and December 9. None of the three has an answer yet.
