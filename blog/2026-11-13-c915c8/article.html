Chip Stocks Slide as Investors Question How AI Expansion Gets Funded

Friday, November 13, ended with a sharp split between major US stock indexes and semiconductor shares. The Dow edged up about 0.1%. The S&P 500 declined 0.4% to 7,561.67, while the Nasdaq Composite fell about 0.9% to 25,136.27. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, or SOX, dropped roughly 3.4%. NVDA fell about 2%, while AMD, AVGO, MRVL and other chip names also faced selling pressure. Interest-rate concerns and Nvidia's abrupt leadership transition both mattered to sentiment, but one headline cannot explain the entire tape.

What makes the selloff interesting is its breadth. Following Jensen Huang's resignation, some traders initially bought Nvidia's competitors as possible beneficiaries of supplier diversification. By Friday those shares were falling too. A plausible concern is that tighter financing for AI-cloud customers could delay equipment orders across the industry. Winning an investor's attention is not the same as winning a funded, deliverable customer order.

Monthly PPI Eased, but Credit Is Not Suddenly Cheap

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on November 13 that October final-demand producer prices increased 0.3% month over month, below expectations near 0.4% and September's revised 1.4% surge. Annual PPI, however, accelerated from 6.3% to 6.5%, its fastest pace since November 2022. Goods prices advanced 0.5% and services rose 0.2%. A softer-than-expected monthly print and persistently high annual inflation are not contradictory facts.

The 10-year Treasury yield briefly reached around 5.17% on Friday. Markets were debating another Federal Reserve rate increase in December. Claims that the Fed has already committed to three consecutive hikes go beyond its published decisions. But for a leveraged data-centre operator, even modestly higher funding costs can change debt-service coverage, project economics and the return expected by its equity investors.

A CEO Transition Is Confirmed. A $500 Billion Loan Book Is Not.

Nvidia announced on November 12 that founder Jensen Huang had resigned as CEO, president and director. CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO, with Harvey Jones appointed chair. An SEC Form 8-K independently records the executive transition. The company said it expects operations, customer commitments and its product roadmap to continue, but investors will now test whether leadership changes its approach to allocating capital and supporting customers.

On August 10, Nvidia announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to build financing platforms intended to mobilize more than $500 billion of third-party capital over time. Its announcement describes memoranda of understanding, still subject to final agreements. It is not a disclosure that $500 billion has already been lent, nor a binding obligation for each of the six partners to fund a particular amount.

Recent reporting has described negotiations over the size of Nvidia's residual-value support, with some financing partners seeking stronger guarantees than the proposed support of up to roughly 25%. Reporting also indicates the new platform had not yet completed its first deal. Claims that a named firm has definitively withdrawn need a formal statement or independently verifiable agreement change. The investable question is what the first legally binding project will look like: who lends, who guarantees, and at what credit spread?

The $530.5 Billion Figure Combines Different Financial Exposures

Nvidia's Form 10-Q as of July 26, 2026 discloses $366 billion of future commitments in five categories: $279 billion in supply and capacity, $29 billion in cloud service agreements, $25 billion in data-centre leases not yet commenced, $25 billion in equity investment commitments, and $8 billion in capital expenditure commitments.

The chart's five bars add to $366 billion, not $530.5 billion. The disclosure also lists $56 billion of separate AI-cloud and third-party lease commitments, plus $108.5 billion of maximum gross guarantees. Adding these headline categories produces $530.5 billion, but it does not produce a single debt balance. They have different terms, accounting treatment, timelines and triggers.

In particular, $105 billion of the maximum guarantee exposure relates to an SB Energy project associated with OpenAI. That support becomes effective in stages as facilities enter service and payments are triggered by defined defaults; it does not mean Nvidia owes $105 billion in cash today. Another disclosed $99 billion represents equity investments already held in its ecosystem. That investment exposure is real, but it is not a new outstanding loan that can be added to future payables without changing the question being measured.

The risk is more subtle than a single enormous debt figure. Customer financing, GPU purchases, lease commitments and infrastructure construction can be economically interdependent. If a customer cannot collect enough revenue, loses refinancing access or sees its equipment's resale value fall, some contingent exposures may become more expensive. Ordinary supplier purchase commitments and guarantees should be stress-tested separately.

Nscale Shows Why Contracted Demand Is Not the Same as Secured Funding

Nvidia-backed infrastructure developer Nscale filed an amended S-1/A on November 12. Its disclosures show approximately $1.686 billion of cash generated from operating activities in the first half of 2026, against approximately $3.286 billion of cash used in investing activities. Those figures do not, by themselves, prove an imminent default: customer prepayments, construction spending and debt transactions operate on different schedules. They do illustrate the capital intensity of the buildout.

Rather than repeat an unsupported claim that interest consumes precisely 68% of revenue, investors should examine the filing's matching periods and definitions. The practical questions are whether the company has enforceable financing for each project, whether power and facilities are delivered when promised, and how prepayments and booked contracts turn into unrestricted operating cash. A contract to supply AI capacity is not a bank's commitment to lend the full construction budget.

This is the mechanism often called circular financing: an AI hardware supplier invests in, or supports, a buyer that in turn purchases its equipment. Such arrangements can help customers enter the market and accelerate product deployment. They become risky when the apparent order growth ultimately depends on repeated external financing rather than paying end users. If actual AI demand converts into stable customer cash flow, the same contracts can perform as designed. Calling every strategic investment a Ponzi scheme would be no more rigorous than treating every booking as cash.

AMD, AVGO, MRVL and TSM Still Need Orders, Not Just a Rotation Narrative

AMD and AVGO initially attracted investors looking for alternatives to Nvidia after the CEO transition. Both sold off alongside the sector on Friday, a reminder that falling industry risk appetite can overwhelm an individual stock's potential relative advantage. For traders using the programme's earlier technical scenarios, AMD's $501–$528 area remains a zone to reassess after a pullback. Broadcom's $335 downside reference and a stronger trend test around $365 are conditional levels, not mechanically executable buy or sell instructions.

MRVL is not an immediate substitute for every Nvidia AI accelerator. Its optical and connectivity exposure depends on customer qualification, design wins and actual product shipments. The earlier $160–$172 region can serve as a technical reference, but evidence of orders and revenue recognition is more durable than a short-term price spike.

TSM has a different position in the supply chain because advanced manufacturing and packaging capacity serve multiple chip designers. That diversity offers resilience but not immunity if data-centre financing slows customer procurement or shifts wafer starts. Levels around $399 and $424, with lower observation zones of $360–$378 and $320–$350, should be paired with customer capital spending and actual shipment guidance. They are not guaranteed floors.

Cisco's November 12 earnings provide a counterweight to an indiscriminate demand-collapse story. CSCO reported fiscal first-quarter revenue of $18.5 billion, up 24% year over year, raised full-year guidance, and increased its expected fiscal 2027 hyperscaler AI infrastructure revenue to $9 billion. Growth in the pace of new orders may be uneven even when the annual revenue outlook improves. Those are distinct metrics, and investors can question future durability without pretending today's sales have disappeared.

Anthropic's IPO Pricing Is Not the Same as Its Market Capitalization

Anthropic's November 10 amended S-1/A proposes a public offering of 825 million Class A shares at an estimated $70–$78 per share. About 801.35 million shares would be newly sold by Anthropic, while roughly 23.65 million come from existing selling shareholders. At $78, the entire public share offering totals about $64.4 billion, not $110 billion. The prospectus also describes a separate $26 billion concurrent strategic private placement conditional on completing the IPO; it should not be confused with the public float.

Valuation estimates ranging from roughly $1 trillion on one share-count basis to $1.4 trillion on a fully diluted basis are not interchangeable with the actual proceeds to Anthropic, the cash received by selling shareholders or the eventual first trading price. The preliminary prospectus does not establish that founders can simply sell 10% of their entire holdings on opening day; that assertion requires checking the actual secondary shares, lockups and exemptions.

The listing will be a meaningful test of investors' appetite for AI-model economics, but the prospectus explicitly remains subject to completion. The more useful long-term question is whether revenue from paying customers can support the cost of computing commitments and expansion. A strong offering could improve confidence across AI financing; a delayed or reduced offering could instead prompt creditors to demand stronger cash evidence from infrastructure borrowers.

Nvidia's products remain competitively important. What has changed is investors' willingness to value every AI funding pledge as though it were already cash. The next checks are the company's November 17 earnings, the first binding financing deal under the proposed $500 billion platform, Nscale's funding progress and Treasury yields. For investors with concentrated semiconductor exposure or written puts, assignment cash requirements and overnight downside deserve attention before any attempt to buy the dip.
