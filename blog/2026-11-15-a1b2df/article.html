The resignation repriced governance before the order book

On November 12, Nvidia's board said cofounder Jensen Huang had resigned as chief executive and as a director, effective immediately. Chief financial officer Colette Kress became interim chief executive and stayed on as chief financial officer. Harvey Jones became the company's first board chair. The board said it would look inside and outside the company for a permanent successor. CNN, Axios, and Business Insider all carried the appointment the same day. The stock traded as low as $148.80 and closed at $166.19, down 4.3% from the November 11 close of $173.73, on about 748 million shares. On November 13 it closed at $162.85 after touching $170.00.

The ledger splits in two. Goldman Sachs analyst James Schneider kept a Buy rating that day and cut his target to $220 from $300. The note, as carried by TheFly, treats the next 12 to 18 months of earnings as cushioned by the existing backlog, so the direct hit to the income statement is described as limited. What got repriced at once was the multiple. Customers, the foundry, and export permissions had often pointed at one person. Contracts can still ship. The earnings multiple waits until a new chief executive resigns those relationships. From the November 6 close of $186.64 to the November 13 close, the stock lost about $24, or 12.7%, in five sessions.

The two ends of the chain do not want the same thing. Nvidia has to show that the roadmap, shipments, and gross margin do not get rewritten because one person left. Microsoft, Google, and Amazon want clusters that can be powered before 2027, not a succession press release. The board wants a search it can explain to shareholders. Kress is signing both as finance chief and as operator, so cash and daily supply sit under one name for now. Customers will not add orders because a statement says operations continue. They will read whether the November 17 outlook still stands behind the pre-resignation order book.

Set against the management shocks around the 1999 telecom spending peak, fiber contracts were still on the books then, and what fell first was the multiple investors would pay for the people running them. The shared piece is that racks ship by the quarter and the trust discount moves by the day. The difference is that brokers this time wrote the cushion as a 12- to 18-month backlog, not dark fiber already in the ground with no paying user on the end. The multiple can fall without revenue recognition collapsing the same day.

The check sits in the next two quarters, not in the name of a permanent chief executive. If November 17 keeps the following two quarters inside the pre-resignation frame, the governance discount is still in the multiple and not yet in orders. If guidance is cut, or management stops describing the backlog as cover for the next 12 to 18 months, that cushion is falsified. An internal memo reported by Business Insider puts an all-hands meeting on November 18. That is a calendar item. It does not replace the numbers.

The shovel seller cut its own target and still wrote a check

On November 10, Anthropic updated its registration statement with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. The offering is 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78. Reuters and TechCrunch both wrote that the top of the range implies a fully diluted value of about $1.4 trillion, and that the offering itself would raise up to about $64.4 billion. The same filing arranges a $26 billion private placement of non-voting Class N shares at the offering price. Buyers include Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft, and TSMC. Both reports put Google and Nvidia at about $10 billion each. Reuters said trading is expected to start on Nasdaq on November 19 under the symbol ANT.

The structure matters more than the valuation headline. On November 12 Nvidia cut its own target from $300 to $220 and, in the same week, agreed to put up about $10 billion as a cornerstone buyer of a model lab's non-voting stock. The chip designer is marking down its own equity and funding a customer's balance sheet at the same time. The chart below keeps only the amounts two independent reports agree on. The other five buyers sum to $6 billion. Their separate checks are not split out.

Profit sits in different places on the chain. Nvidia, TSMC, and Broadcom earn the gross margin on chips, wafers, and interconnects. Anthropic receives cash it can spend on compute, payroll, and the next training run. The supplier's check lands on the customer's books, and a large part of it can come back as capital spending on the supplier's cards. The prospectus writes one round of AI spending as two entries. Depreciation still sits with whoever buys the accelerators, not with whoever writes the weights. Non-voting also means the $10 billion buys a financial exposure, not a seat on the board.

Physical delivery did not stop for the governance headline. The board said customer commitments and the product roadmap continue. If that holds, advanced packaging, liquid-cooled racks, and signed clusters still follow the old contracts. The governance discount hits the multiple on NVDA. It does not hit a rack that is already ordered. If the search runs past two quarters, more of the next orders could shift toward accelerators AMD and AVGO already sell. That is share moving, not demand vanishing on November 12. TSM does not gain a packaging line because of a resignation, and it does not lose one because of a placement.

Two things in the next two or three quarters can falsify this. First, whether Nvidia completes the roughly $10 billion Class N purchase at the amount in the filing. Second, whether capital-return language after November 17 treats the money as customer financing rather than a financial investment unrelated to supply. If the placement is cut, or the report stops calling the backlog a 12- to 18-month cushion, this section has to change.

2 · EARNINGS

Model revenue is rising, and the valuation still leans on suppliers

The same November 10 update said Anthropic's run-rate revenue reached $81 billion as of October 31. Reuters and TechCrunch both cite that figure. After a financing round in May 2026 the company was valued at $965 billion, a number that matches in TechCrunch and Business Insider. The top of this range is about $1.4 trillion fully diluted. On an undiluted basis Reuters put the value at about $1 trillion. Revenue is higher. The offering value did not follow the richer targets that circulated earlier. It sits in a range suppliers were willing to buy at the offering price.

Eighty-one billion dollars of run-rate revenue is API and enterprise contracts still running, not free cash already in the bank. A fully diluted $1.4 trillion is about 17 times that run rate. An undiluted $1 trillion is about 12 times. The multiple buys the claim that revenue can keep stepping up for several more quarters. It does not buy profits already earned. Suppliers buying non-voting shares at the offering price write part of the demand side in as shareholders early. What enterprise customers still pay for is inference volume, evaluation, permissions, and human review after a mistake. Those costs do not leave the contract because Google or Nvidia bought Class N shares.

The incentives pull in different directions. Google is putting up about $10 billion at the offering price and also selling its own models. Nvidia sells cards to every lab and, at the same price, holds a financial exposure to Anthropic. A product that is only a thin interface gets its margin squeezed by model price cuts and by the cloud vendors' own agents. What remains is permissions, audit, and where the data sits inside a workflow, not a rank on a leaderboard. Each time open weights get closer to a closed model, another layer of usage premium comes off. A supplier's equity check does not lock that premium in place.

Set against the cloud price cuts of the early 2010s, compute and storage prices were marked down again and again, software margins were repriced, and what remained was workflow and data location. The parallel is that model calls are becoming metered infrastructure. The difference is that chip suppliers now appear on the customer's shareholder list, so price pressure and capital ties happen together. The dark fiber of 1999 was supply ahead of demand. This time demand is still signing clusters, and the equity line writes buyer and seller into the same prospectus.

The next two quarters only need two items already in the documents. One is whether the next revenue disclosure after listing still supports the October 31 snapshot of $81 billion. The other is whether the $70 to $78 range is formally changed. On November 11 the Financial Times, citing people familiar with the process, said bankers were discussing a price above $78. That is a report, not a filing. If the final price is below $70, or run-rate revenue falls clearly off the path in the prospectus, the line that revenue is rising while suppliers hold up the valuation has to be rewritten.

3 · FOCUS

Tuesday checks the order book, not the successor's name

The week split AI capital spending into two sheets. One is Nvidia's orders and gross margin. The November 12 resignation did not strike them out of the contracts, and the November 13 close of $162.85 did not finish below the $148.80 low. The other sheet is the multiple investors will pay for a roadmap still explained by the same person. That sheet changed the day of the resignation. Anthropic's prospectus shows the companies that sell the cards using their own cash to become shareholders of the model lab at the public offering price.

Over the next two quarters, three numbers can be checked. Whether Nvidia's outlook on November 17 and after still sits inside the pre-resignation frame. Whether the roughly $10 billion Class N purchase is completed at the amount in the filing. Whether Anthropic's next revenue figure after listing still matches the October 31 run rate of $81 billion. If two of the three fail, "the orders are still there and the multiple moved first" becomes "the orders are loosening too." The first-day move in ANT on November 19 does not answer those three.

The public point fits in one line. A founder leaving changes the multiple. A supplier buying in changes where the cash on the customer's balance sheet comes from. Neither has rewritten clusters that are already signed. Watching only listing day misses the harder check, which is whether management still stands behind that order book on November 17.
