A Break Below 7,500 Overwhelmed Wednesday's Rally

The most revealing fact from the week of November 2 was that Treasury yields fell while technology shares sold off. According to the Associated Press's November 6 closing report, the S&P 500 ended the week at 7,376.86, down 2.68% from 7,580.07 on October 30. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 3.37%, the Dow slipped 0.94%, and the Russell 2000 fell 2.05%. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, or SOX, lost about 12.15%. SPY, QQQ and IWM track different equity benchmarks, so their ETF returns should not be confused with the index-level figures.

Last Monday's weekly note treated 7,580 as the S&P 500's first decision level and 7,500 as the next defensive area. On November 4, a 1.58% rally lifted the index to 7,688.62. That apparent recovery broke down the following day. On November 5, the S&P 500 plunged 4.36% to 7,353.73. Friday's 0.31% rebound only brought it to 7,376.86, still below both levels. The prior plan identified the risk of a break beneath 7,500 but underestimated how quickly it could happen. Anyone treating that number as a guaranteed floor learned an expensive lesson.

Cross-asset markets underscore the distinction between lower rates and better risk appetite. The 10-year Treasury yield moved from roughly 4.93% on October 30 to 4.86% on November 6, a decline of seven basis points. The two-year benchmark fell from about 4.56% to 4.51%. The dollar index climbed about 0.4%, from 101.88 to 102.30. Brent crude fell around 13.3%, from $113.43 to $98.32 using comparable futures observations. Meanwhile, the VIX rose from 19.42 to 25.64. Lower oil prices and Treasury yields were not enough to stabilize growth stocks.

AI Funding and Earnings Risk Overpowered the Bond-Market Relief

The market moved in opposing directions on successive sessions. Following the November 3 midterm elections, Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. On November 4, the Dow gained 3.03% and the S&P 500 rose 1.58%, while money appeared to rotate from chips into established cloud platforms and selected cyclical names. On November 5, heavy selling returned to technology. The Nasdaq Composite lost 5.52% that day; SOX finished the week down more than 12%. Neither election outcomes nor any single headline can explain each stock move on its own. Falling crude prices, the unwinding of hedges, crowded positioning and funding concerns all interacted.

The financing transmission mechanism is easiest to see in data centres. Texas's newly elected governor proposed a pause in new data-centre development during the campaign, amid concerns about electricity costs, water and permits. A campaign pledge is not an enacted construction ban. But if approvals slip, customers may delay accepting GPUs, optical equipment, power systems and other hardware. Demand may remain intact while shipment timing and collections move into later quarters. Operators financed with debt still pay interest as projects await approval. That creates pressure on their cash runway and can eventually spill into suppliers' working capital.

Existing cloud providers such as MSFT, AMZN and GOOGL may enjoy a relative advantage because they already operate facilities and serve customers. However, those companies are building additional campuses too, so they are not immune to restrictions. Project-by-project permits and company spending guidance matter far more than a sweeping claim that US data-centre construction has stopped.

ORCL illustrates why the funding schedule now matters as much as AI demand. On October 28, it outlined an incremental $20 billion financing allowance through fiscal 2030. On November 2, it priced roughly $10 billion of equity and $10 billion of bonds at once. Oracle stayed within the announced limit, but effectively used the whole allowance up front. Investors must therefore reassess the gap between immediate spending and eventual cash receipts. AI bookings are not free cash flow, and capital expenditure and receivables cannot be ignored.

The October employment report, released November 6, added a monetary-policy constraint. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported 165,000 additional payroll jobs and a 4.1% unemployment rate. Reuters reported a pre-release consensus around 65,000 jobs, and market-implied odds of a December Federal Reserve rate increase near 78% after the release. The Fed's policy target, last set in October, remains 3.75% to 4.00%. A flight into longer-dated Treasuries does not automatically mean easier credit or lower refinancing costs for leveraged AI developers.

There are two fundamentally different paths to lower yields. Falling inflation with stable earnings can help equity valuations. Investors seeking safety while analysts cut earnings forecasts can also drive yields down, but that environment is usually much less friendly to growth stocks. Last week's tape looked closer to the second pattern. A few basis points of relief on Treasuries will not repair weak cash conversion or a widening corporate credit spread.

2 · MACRO

CPI and Infrastructure Earnings Are the Week's Main Tests

The review period ends November 8. The following events are scheduled for November 9–13 and are not yet reported results.

On Monday, November 9, TTWO and OXY are among companies expected to report after the close, adding perspective on consumer discretionary spending and the energy cycle. Tuesday, November 10, is the more consequential macro date. At 8:30 a.m. Eastern, the Bureau of Labor Statistics is due to release October CPI and real earnings. Oil's collapse may ease future energy inflation, but it would be premature to assume services and shelter inflation have already slowed.

On Tuesday after the close, CRWV holds its third-quarter earnings call at 5:00 p.m. Eastern. This is a direct test of AI data-centre funding. Revenue growth will matter, but so will capital expenditures, operating cash flow, the pace of contract delivery and the cost and availability of secured borrowing. Strong reported sales accompanied by persistent construction-related cash burn may not reassure investors.

Thursday, November 12, brings results from CSCO for fiscal Q1 2027 and AMAT for fiscal Q4 and full-year 2026, with both calls scheduled for 4:30 p.m. Eastern. Cisco can illuminate networking orders and the buildout of AI connectivity. Applied Materials can help reveal whether manufacturers are slowing spending on equipment. NBIS also appears on third-party earnings calendars for Thursday; verify the issuer's final announcement before treating that timing as fixed.

Finally, October PPI is due Friday, November 13, at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Softer CPI and PPI, alongside steadier infrastructure cash flow, could offer the two pillars a durable tech rebound needs: less pressure on discount rates and more confidence in earnings. Hotter inflation and resilient jobs might revive rate concerns. Even if inflation cools, weaker cash-flow guidance from CoreWeave or equipment vendors could keep technology under pressure.

3 · SEMIS

The Market Must Reclaim 7,500 Before Risk Can Be Added Back

The S&P 500 finished November 6 at 7,376.86. Its first task is now to stop setting new lows around the 7,350–7,370 area, then recover 7,500. Regaining 7,580 with broader Nasdaq and semiconductor participation would provide stronger evidence that a rebound has become more durable. Another sustained break beneath the November 5 low of 7,353.73 would favour portfolio defence over aggressive dip-buying. These are observation levels, not guaranteed executable stops.

QQQ lagged broad equities last week. The Russell 2000 and equal-weight shares also fell but held up somewhat better. Follow market breadth, semiconductor relative strength, Treasury yields, the VIX and credit spreads together. A one-day rally in Microsoft or Amazon is insufficient evidence of a broad recovery. Also remember that SOXX completed a three-for-one share split around November 5: pre-split and post-split ETF prices must be adjusted before drawing technical conclusions.

If CPI cools, equipment orders stabilize and the index holds above 7,500 for several sessions, reducing some protective hedges becomes easier to justify. If inflation surprises on the upside while the VIX stays elevated and semiconductors keep leading declines, exposure to expensive or heavily financed growth assets deserves restraint, even if Treasury yields fall further. Investors holding large technology positions or short put options should first calculate possible assignment cash needs, sector concentration and a tolerable one-month drawdown. Protective puts and collars can help, but premiums and upside caps are real costs.

Last week's central warning has now been tested: the 10-year yield moved below 5%, and technology stocks still were not safe. The next dividing line is whether cooling inflation arrives together with improving AI cash conversion. Without better collections and executable customer orders, another rally may only be a pause in the repricing.
