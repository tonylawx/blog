00 · PREFACE

Preface

I've long felt that when someone shows strong signs of narcissistic personality disorder (NPD), looking back at their family can reveal some familiar patterns.

That doesn't mean everyone with NPD had a parent with NPD, or that a personality disorder is simply passed down. But the ways we learn to relate to people as children can be far more stubborn than we think.

My understanding of NPD didn't begin with online stories about toxic partners or bosses. It began with my mother.

More precisely, it began when I realized I was becoming more and more like her.

01 · REFLECTION

The habits I hated became my own

My mother shows many traits commonly associated with NPD.

There seems to be only one rule in her world: she is always right, and everyone else is expected to revolve around her.

She can lose her temper, dismiss people, and make demands whenever she wants, while showing little concern for how anyone else feels. You can't argue back or even try to reason with her. For her, the point isn't who's right or wrong. The point is whether you obey. When an argument isn't going her way, she can pull rank simply because she's the elder.

I hated living with that as a child.

So when I grew up, I consciously tried to do the opposite.

She was emotional, so I pushed myself to be rational and objective. She put people down, so I tried to talk things through. She wouldn't admit mistakes, so I learned to revisit conflicts and sort out what happened. I wanted everything to be clear.

For a while, I thought I'd escaped her influence completely.

Then I noticed I had developed a pretty strong case of what people in Chinese call "dad energy"—that overbearing habit of lecturing others.

When someone has a problem, I can't resist analysing it. When they make a choice I think is wrong, I want to fix it. Even an ordinary conversation can turn into me speaking from a position of superiority.

Sometimes I even catch myself thinking, "I'm trying to help. Why won't they listen?"

But isn't that another form of control?

My mother uses emotion to make people comply. I use logic to persuade people to accept my judgment.

She assumes she's always right. I assume I see things more clearly.

I rejected her explosive behaviour, but unknowingly inherited the arrogance underneath it.

That brings me to a harsh expression I sometimes use to mock myself: "支性难改"—an ugly, deeply ingrained set of habits that is hard to shake.

The term itself is insulting. I use it as self-criticism because it forces me to face things about myself I'd rather not admit.

The urge to lecture. Double standards. Poor boundaries. The need to make decisions for other people.

And there's a subtle mindset underneath it all: I can criticize you, but you mustn't question me. Everything I do is for your own good, so you should accept it.

A couple of days ago, I posted something on WeChat Moments:

"Stay away from spiders and weaver women, and you'll have a peaceful life."

A lot of people didn't understand it. They wondered why I would suddenly write something like that.

I wasn't simply repeating the online stereotypes attached to "spider" and "weaver woman", nor was I trying to reduce a gender or a whole group to a caricature.

These are derogatory Chinese internet labels with a history of targeting groups of people. What I wanted to criticize was a pattern I'd repeatedly encountered: extreme self-centredness, constant lecturing, double standards, no respect for boundaries, treating other people's generosity as an entitlement, and refusing to carry one's own responsibilities.

Above all, it's the conviction that one can never be wrong and everyone else should always make the adjustment.

What I want to stay away from is that suffocating way of relating to people, not somebody's identity.

Then I had a more uncomfortable thought.

I was telling people online to stay away from "spiders" and "weaver women" while recognizing some of the same flaws in myself.

My own condescending tone. My need to explain everything. My instinct to correct and control others.

That's the part I find hardest to face.

It's easy to criticize someone else. Admitting that you share the same problems is much harder.

And that's what makes family patterns so frightening.

You may not look or sound like your parents, but you can inherit the operating system they used in their relationships.

You can become financially independent, move to another city, even cut off contact. If the underlying pattern remains, you haven't truly escaped.

I used to have trouble understanding the phrase "psychologically killing the father and mother."

Now I think I understand it a little better.

It doesn't mean hurting or rejecting your parents. It means dismantling the parent whose voice has taken up residence in your mind.

You have to question the standards, the need for control, and the grandiosity they passed down—even the qualities you've always been proud of.

Take rationality, for example.

Being rational is good. But if you use it to place yourself above others, and treat anyone who rejects your analysis as foolish, your rationality is only arrogance wearing better clothes.

Real awakening isn't proving you're more correct than your parents. It's no longer needing to correct everyone else to feel correct.

You can recognize a problem without pointing it out. You can disagree with someone's choice and still respect their right to make it.

Other people have the right to make mistakes. They also have the right not to take your advice.

These ideas sound simple, but I'm only really learning them as an adult.

Maybe that's what starting over means.

It's not about changing how you speak. It's about questioning the entire way you learned to think.

02 · REFLECTION

The most frightening part is control without self-awareness

NPD is discussed everywhere online. People who are selfish, emotionally unstable, or unwilling to apologize are often labelled with it.

But NPD is a clinical diagnosis that requires professional assessment. You can't diagnose someone from a few arguments.

What I want to examine here are entrenched narcissistic and controlling behaviours that can seriously damage relationships.

1. Never admitting they're wrong

Ordinary people can be stubborn too. They may refuse to back down when they're angry. But once things cool off, there's at least a possibility of facing the facts and admitting a mistake.

Some people seem unable to allow that possibility.

Show them evidence and they'll say you're attacking them. Point out a problem and they'll say you're disrespecting them. Even when they're clearly responsible, the conversation somehow returns to "You made me do it" or "I wouldn't have acted like this if it weren't for you."

They can find fault with you, with other people, with the entire world—just not with themselves.

The most difficult part isn't simply that they won't reason with you. It's that they have a whole self-contained explanation that turns every mistake into somebody else's responsibility.

2. Extreme self-centredness

In these relationships, emotional consideration only seems to flow in one direction.

If they're upset, the whole family must adjust. If you're upset, you're sensitive, dramatic, or unreasonable.

Their needs are urgent. Yours can always wait.

They demand unconditional understanding while rarely putting themselves in your position.

The clearest sign is how they treat other people's effort as something they're entitled to.

Help them ten times and they may barely notice. Say no on the eleventh, and suddenly you've let them down.

3. Treating people like tools

You don't measure empathy by how many nice things somebody says when they're happy. You see it when your feelings come into conflict with their interests.

Some people can be warm, generous, even thoughtful—until something doesn't go their way.

Then they may turn on you immediately.

It doesn't matter whether you're their partner, friend, or even an older family member.

Other people stop looking like independent human beings. They become tools for maintaining that person's emotional comfort and sense of importance.

Agree with them and you're treated well. Disagree, and suddenly you're the enemy.

An actual chat record

What the person I trusted most in the world said to me when I was facing death.

4. Once they're calm, you're expected to forget everything

This may be the most suffocating part for me.

There might have been a terrible argument the day before. They said cruel things and caused real harm.

The next day, they talk to you normally and ask for a favour as though nothing happened.

No apology. No explanation. No effort to repair the relationship.

Try to calmly discuss yesterday's argument and they get angry all over again.

In their mind, they're no longer angry, so the matter is closed.

Whether you're still hurting doesn't matter.

To put it harshly:

When they're upset, you must take it. When they're calm, you must go back to obeying them.

You're not even allowed to hold on to your own feelings. Emotional manipulation can get so intense that you start asking yourself, "Am I not a person too? What did I ever do to deserve this?"

5. The person who must always be right

Some people seem compelled to contradict everything you say.

Whatever the subject, they'll find a flaw just to prove they know better.

In close relationships, that habit can become control over every part of your life.

Your friends, career, clothes, meals, tone of voice—even trivial everyday choices—become things they feel entitled to supervise.

The problem isn't whether their advice is correct. It's whether they can accept that you might not follow it.

Real advice leaves room for a no.

Control doesn't.

The logic of control is simple: if you don't do it my way, you're wrong.

I have to watch for this in myself.

Sometimes I tell myself I'm helping someone analyse a situation when I'm really looking for an intellectual sense of superiority.

I want them to acknowledge that my judgment is right.

If they don't follow my suggestion, I can even feel a little irritated.

Underneath the surface, that isn't so different from my mother's need for control.

6. Why do we keep trying to save them?

People often think that once you recognize someone's behaviour for what it is, you should simply leave.

Real life isn't that simple.

Family ties, love, finances, and emotional dependence can keep someone in an exhausting relationship for years.

There's also a group of people who fall into a "rescuer" role.

They believe that with enough patience and rational explanation, they can finally change the other person.

I used to be like that.

When someone wouldn't listen to reason, my first instinct wasn't to walk away. It was to find another way to explain.

I thought that if I analysed the issue clearly enough, the other person would eventually understand.

But some arguments don't continue because the reasoning isn't clear. They continue because one person never intended to have an equal conversation.

The more you try to correct them, the more they feel their authority is being challenged.

The more you try to fix the relationship, the worse it can become.

Eventually, everyone is exhausted.

Not every problem is yours to solve, and not everyone is yours to save.

Sometimes the only thing you need to change is your own obsession with steering somebody back onto what you think is the right path.

And the problem didn't begin with you. Yet by trying to manage all the consequences, you can end up enabling it.

03 · REFLECTION

When the money runs out, people leave too

I once came across a provocative claim:

"People with NPD will inevitably go bankrupt."

Taken literally, that simply isn't true.

Some people with serious narcissistic traits are professionally successful, even wealthy.

But there's still something worth examining here. For people driven by extreme vanity, unable to reflect on their mistakes, and constantly drawing on other people's resources, financial and relational collapse can become a self-reinforcing cycle.

1. Generous in public, stingy at home

Some people care enormously about how outsiders see them.

They'll spend freely on dinners, gifts, and appearances.

Call them successful, generous, or impressive, and they'll spend even more to maintain that image.

Yet when they get home and face ordinary family responsibilities, they can become remarkably tight-fisted.

Praise from outsiders feeds their vanity. Their family's needs seem like expenses with no applause attached.

I've always found that contradiction absurd.

Someone else often ends up paying for the performance of "saving face."

The danger gets worse when inflated self-confidence meets an inability to admit mistakes.

They look down on slow, steady accumulation. They believe they're special and will eventually strike it rich overnight.

Impulsive investing, chasing trends, taking on too much debt, or even gambling can become expressions of that mindset.

When they make money, it's proof of their brilliance.

When they lose money, it's the market, the environment, or someone who supposedly set them up.

It's never their own judgment.

And if you can't admit a mistake, it's hard to learn from it.

One loss can lead to a bigger gamble. They borrow from one place to cover another until even a comfortable financial position starts to fall apart.

2. Living off others while acting superior

If vanity creates a financial black hole, habitual taking creates one in relationships.

On the Chinese internet, "weaver woman" is sometimes used as a derogatory label for women perceived as materialistic, hypocritical, or demanding.

The term carries gender-based hostility. I don't believe greed or exploitation belongs to any one gender.

What deserves criticism is the mindset that people of any gender can have:

My money is mine, and your money should also be available to me.

Your sacrifices are expected. Mine deserve applause.

When their own resources fall short, they begin drawing on the people around them.

Some are particularly good at presenting themselves as victims.

Even if their own decisions created the problem, they can tell the story as though they've been wronged.

Then they expect family members, partners, or friends to clean up the mess.

The strangest part is that they may remain condescending while depending on other people.

They take resources from someone else and still expect that person to feel grateful.

That's what the Chinese phrase "软饭硬吃" gets at: being supported by someone else while acting as though you're the one in charge.

Sometimes I think two contradictory impulses coexist in the same person:

The appetite of a robber, and the sense of responsibility of a beggar.

They feel entitled when taking, but have a thousand excuses when it's time to be accountable.

3. When every source of support has been drained

The real danger isn't only whether someone goes broke. It's that they can drag other people down with them.

Someone who once had a stable life may keep bailing them out through kindness, obligation, or faith that they'll eventually change.

The first loan is to help them through a hard time.

The second rescue comes with the hope that they've finally learned their lesson.

By the third or fourth, it's hard to tell whether you're helping or enabling.

Slowly, your money is gone, your energy is gone, and your own life begins to suffer.

Meanwhile, the person being rescued may become even more demanding.

After all, they've grown used to having someone else carry the consequences.

But no resource is infinite.

When relatives stop giving money, partners stop compromising, and friends stop believing promises, the people who once surrounded them begin to leave.

The people they treated like endless supplies of emotional and financial support finally say no.

Only then might they realize how much trust has already been used up.

To me, that kind of collapse is even more frightening than financial bankruptcy.

You can earn money again.

But after exhausting people's trust, patience, and affection, another chance may not come.

Of course, this isn't the inevitable fate of everyone with NPD. Personality patterns can change, and people who accept treatment, reflect, and take responsibility may build healthier lives.

What I'm really saying is this:

If a person never reflects, rejects responsibility, and treats everyone around them as an endless resource, they may destroy not only their own life but also the lives of people who genuinely tried to help.

That's why I no longer think distancing yourself from someone who continually drains you is cold-hearted.

Sometimes you have to stop volunteering to step in the same mess.

You can feel compassion for somebody's circumstances without agreeing to spend your life paying off their debts.

04 · REFLECTION

Don't make a child spend a lifetime healing from childhood

That brings me to another question.

Why are more and more young people choosing not to marry or have children?

There are many reasons: financial pressure, the cost of living, personal priorities, and different ideas about marriage and family.

But I think some people are especially cautious about parenthood because they know what an unhealthy family relationship feels like.

They know how much harm an emotionally immature parent can cause.

I sometimes ask myself what would happen if I had a child now.

Could I promise not to pass on my need for control?

If my child ignored my advice, could I genuinely respect that choice?

If they made a decision I considered wrong, would I interfere while insisting it was "for their own good"?

Would I feel that because I worked hard to raise them, they owed me obedience?

And if they challenged my control, would I react like my mother and accuse them of being ungrateful?

Those questions make it difficult for me to casually talk myself into having a child.

Because I know I still have problems of my own.

The arrogance, the overbearing "dad energy," and the desire for control that I hated so much haven't vanished just because I recognize them.

I have to keep reminding myself.

Stop turning every conversation into a lecture.

Stop assuming your judgment is always better.

Stop treating a rejected suggestion as a personal insult.

And stop dressing up control as rationality.

I used to think I was the one rebelling against my family background.

Then I realized there were plenty of things inside me that I also needed to rebel against.

If I haven't freed myself from those patterns, how can I be sure I won't pass them on to another generation?

We don't get to choose our parents.

We have to grow up with whoever they are and however they treat us.

If we're raised by someone profoundly self-centred, controlling, or emotionally unstable, we may spend years of adult life dealing with the consequences.

The cruel irony is that some people spend their whole lives trying to escape their parents, only to inflict similar pain on their own children.

Put two people with the toxic behaviours mocked online as "spider" and "weaver woman" together, and what kind of environment might their children grow up in? It's not a comforting thought.

Then the next generation rebels, leaves, and tries to heal from childhood.

Again and again.

I don't want that to happen through me.

Someone might object: no parent is perfect. Does having flaws mean you shouldn't have children?

Of course not.

You don't need to become a perfect person before you can be a parent.

But I think you should at least face a few questions honestly.

Can you love a child who doesn't turn out the way you hoped?

If they aren't high-achieving or obedient, and eventually choose a life completely unlike the one you planned, can you still respect them?

Can you take responsibility for raising them without placing your emotions, regrets, and unrealized ambitions on their shoulders?

Most importantly, when you hurt them, can you admit it, instead of doing what your own parents did and shifting the blame onto the child?

If you refuse even to consider those questions, I find it hard to call the decision to have a child responsible.

That is especially true for people who know they have serious emotional difficulties but casually believe that having a baby will fix everything.

A child isn't a tool for repairing a marriage, a retirement plan, or an accessory to a parent's ego.

A child is a person.

They don't owe it to their parents to carry problems the adults refuse to confront.

I also don't believe choosing not to have children makes anyone inherently more virtuous. People can face their past, learn healthier ways to love, and become better parents than their own.

But for me, until I can build healthier and more equal relationships, I don't want to bring another life into the world lightly.

I don't want a child to spend their early years resisting me, only to grow up and suddenly realize:

The things they hated most have already become part of them.

Maybe that's why, deep down, you've already made your own choice to remain unmarried or child-free.

Do you really want your child to spend their whole life healing from childhood?

They didn't ask to be born. Why should they carry your unresolved pain?

To me, "psychologically killing the father and mother" doesn't mean cutting off your parents or denying your past.

It means admitting that the things that hurt you may now be part of your own personality.

Then you try, piece by piece, to pull them out.

Even if it takes years, even if it's painful, even if you must question yourself over and over.

I've come to believe that recognizing a problem doesn't mean you've solved it.

The hard part is making a conscious effort, in every relationship that follows, not to repeat it.

When I first encountered the phrase "支性难改", I mostly thought about other people.

Now I think the person I need to watch most closely is myself.

It's easy to stand above others and mock them with labels like "spider" and "weaver woman."

What's hard is admitting that you, too, can be controlling, selfish, and arrogant—and then actually changing.

I don't want to spend my life fighting to be different from my mother, only to become another version of her.

If one day I can loosen the grip of those habits, respect other people's boundaries, and stop relying on control to feel secure, maybe that will be the day I've truly escaped.

As for whether to have children, I think that should be a thoughtful personal choice, not a duty imposed by age.

If I can't do it responsibly, I'd rather let the cycle stop with me.

Not because I'm especially noble.

But because I know exactly how that pain feels.

The suffering I went through doesn't have to become an innocent child's childhood.
