A Seven-Cent Quote Still Needs to Produce Usable Work

On November 5, Google announced Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash's general availability in its developer blog and Cloud release. It is offered through the Gemini API and Google Cloud. Standard generation and editing now support clips up to 15 seconds, versus 10 seconds previously. The announced approximate price for 720p output with synchronized sound is $0.07 per second, about 30% lower. The Decoder confirmed the price and duration terms that day. Those are changes to a purchasable service, not proof of a 30% reduction in the full cost of a usable clip. Quality checks, rework, and postproduction still count, and selling prices do not disclose Google's inference costs.

The token rates make the calculation clearer. Both generations' 720p output with sound uses 5,792 tokens per second, priced at $17.50 and $12 per million output tokens respectively. That puts a 10-second output at about $1.0136 on the old model and $0.69504 on the new one. A new 15-second output costs about $1.04256. These are standard-price output charges. Input, text output, upscaling, and postproduction are additional; synchronized sound is included. Comparing an old 10-second clip with a new 15-second clip cannot establish a price increase. A fair price comparison holds duration and resolution constant, with retries adding further charges.

Buyers also need to count how much generation meets a fixed quality standard. For this output charge alone, roughly 46% more cumulative generation than on the old model would offset the reduction from $17.50 to $12. That is a calculated pricing threshold, not an observed rework rate, and it excludes labor. Revisions to shot continuity, branding, or character consistency can change the outcome. A model provider cuts usage prices first. Whether platforms pass on the discount and customers ultimately spend less depends on subscriptions, billing terms, and acceptance standards. The API discount cannot simply be treated as an equal improvement in profit.

Earlier cloud price cuts offer a useful comparison. On March 26, 2014, AWS announced its 42nd reduction, covering compute, storage, and databases. Customers still had to configure resources, manage data, and operate software. Cheaper infrastructure left total spending dependent on consumption and operations. Generative work adds uncertainty about quality: the same request does not always yield something usable. Lower inference prices can make experimentation more affordable. They do not establish an equivalent reduction in human review or production work, or reveal an improvement in Google's own compute costs or gross margin.

Over the next 2 to 3 quarters, buyers should hold clip length, resolution, and quality standards fixed, then track total generated seconds, first-pass acceptance, and editing time. Mode restrictions matter too. Standard generation and editing reach 15 seconds, while interpolation between the first and last frames and individual extensions remain within 3 to 10 seconds. Audio references still require allowlisting, unlike synchronized sound already included in output charges. Wider use becomes supportable when conditions are comparable and the full cost per accepted clip falls. More API calls without lower costs for usable output do not establish greater productivity.

Billing Starts While Construction Still Needs Funding

On November 5, Core Scientific's third-quarter results and 10-Q reported 485 MW of customer capacity in service and billable at September 30, up from 395 MW at June 30, an increase of 90 MW on the same basis. Third-quarter colocation revenue was about $215.4 million, up roughly 58% sequentially. Of the 590 MW leased to CoreWeave, 105 MW remained under construction or commissioning. Yahoo Finance and Benzinga also covered the capacity and revenue progress. These are quarter-end figures, not capacity built on November 5. The full 590 MW is not yet in service. Billable capacity establishes neither equipment utilization nor receipt of all rent that day.

The chart shows company-reported capacity in service and billable at each period end on a consistent basis. It does not measure GPU utilization, model throughput, or all contracted capacity. Dividing total quarterly capital expenditure by the 90 MW increase would not establish construction cost per megawatt.

Construction spending and service receipts need separate checks. Third-quarter capital expenditure was about $985 million, including property and equipment, land, and development rights across project stages. Dividing that figure by the additional 90 MW would not produce a valid unit construction cost. For the first nine months, net operating cash inflow was about $296 million, against $1.495 billion spent on property and equipment, before separately recorded asset acquisitions. Operating inflow included sales of self-mined digital assets and cannot all be called AI colocation rent. Quarterly capital expenditure and year-to-date cash flow also differ in definition and period; subtracting one from the other would be misleading.

More billable capacity demonstrates delivery progress; cash recovery still depends on contracts. The 10-Q says customer electricity costs pass through without a markup, with revenue and colocation costs recorded gross. Revenue growth therefore does not imply equivalent growth in high-margin rent. About $3.2 billion of net financing inflow in the first nine months also shows substantial external funding alongside operations and construction. The provider must arrange power, retrofits, and delivery, while customers separately bear chip and model-running costs. Without comparable tariffs, cooling efficiency, and customer loads, 485 MW cannot be translated into a full cost per million tokens.

A 2001 Federal Reserve speech on capital investment and 2003 Richmond Fed telecom research help explain the timing risk. Both examined mismatches among long-haul fiber expansion, last-mile connections, and demand expectations. Infrastructure can eventually prove useful without repaying its original investors promptly. Excess capacity in one segment can coexist with insufficient delivery elsewhere. Core Scientific, however, reports actual billable capacity and has lease contracts, different demand evidence from idle fiber. The useful historical comparison concerns the sequence of construction and cash recovery. It cannot by itself predict collapsing AI demand.

For CORZ, watch the conversion of contracted capacity into billing and whether rental receipts gradually cover construction needs. Through Q2 and Q3 2027, the remaining 105 MW entering billing on project schedules, rising recurring receipts, and less reliance on fresh financing would support better delivery. Contracts growing while billing stalls, construction spending rises, and debt burdens expand would weaken near-term expectations. This does not require construction to stop now. Orders, service commencement, revenue, and cash each need verification. Progress at one stage cannot be booked prematurely as an earnings commitment.

Addressing a Review Comment Is Not Completing a Fix

On November 5, GitHub added code-review details and an addressed rate to its Copilot usage metrics API. Organizations and enterprises can inspect comment handling by repository and day. Applying a suggestion, editing manually, and asking Copilot to make a change all count as action. So can replying that the issue will be fixed in another pull request later. A pull request is a code change submitted for team review and merging; handling a comment does not guarantee a deployed patch. BuildApps and other publishers checked the update but offered no independently measured improvement in remediation efficiency or cost.

The denominator has timing and sample limits. Comments are evaluated when their pull request merges, leaving difficult, still-open changes outside that day's denominator. Bot-initiated pull requests are generally excluded from comment evaluation. A separate count of merged pull requests with no human request for changes after the first Copilot review still includes bots. Dividing those measures cannot yield a human-replacement rate. Buyers and engineering leaders need comparable repositories, change risks, and merge periods. A higher daily addressed rate alone does not establish greater model accuracy or an equivalent reduction in human work.

Costs require a link from those actions to actual resources. Beyond model calls and tool credits, people read comments, assess false positives, change code, run regression tests, and handle rollbacks or repairs after deployment. GitHub's release gives neither those hours nor total cost per accepted change. The API's user-period AI-credit consumption field is not broken out for code review, so it cannot all be allocated to that feature. The platform records activity; teams verify outcomes. Rewarding only the addressed rate could make deferred-fix replies improve a report without shortening the time to a real fix.

This problem predates generative AI. Google's 2018 paper on static-analysis tools revisited a 2009 FindBugs exercise: 3,954 warnings were reviewed and 640 were fixed, about 16% of the reviewed sample. That was a particular historical workflow, not today's Copilot fix rate. Finding, recording, acting on, and fixing an issue have long been different states. Integrating tools into code review can get findings seen. Developer trust in the warnings, whether a fix justifies the effort, and how work is accepted still determine their practical value.

Over the next 2 to 3 quarters, track similar changes and test human review minutes, merge-to-deployment time, rollbacks, and urgent repairs together. A higher addressed rate with less acceptance work, faster delivery, and no increase in failures would support a larger automated-review budget. More deferred replies, or complex changes staying outside the denominator, would not. These measures provide testable conditions. They do not show how much engineering labor has already been saved or how many defects have been prevented. No human request for changes cannot be reported as no human review.

Measure Savings in Accepted Results over the Next 2 to 3 Quarters

Google's output prices, Core Scientific's billable capacity, and GitHub's addressed rate each document part of cost or delivery. Businesses increasingly need to explain how those resources turn into accepted results. Price tables describe usage charges, data-center billing identifies a service stage, and addressed rates record developer actions. None replaces the customer's complete business accounts. Keeping those boundaries clear helps identify technological gains without dismissing the price cuts and delivery progress already reported.

Model price competition will push procurement comparisons toward total cost per usable output. Through Q2 and Q3 2027, the tests are cumulative generation, human review time, and completion costs at a consistent quality standard. Sustained improvements could support wider use and renewals. Rework, platform charges, or human effort absorbing the discount would weaken the case for larger budgets, even though unit prices fell. Generation need not suit every task, and higher output counts do not establish greater willingness to pay.

Funding and engineering acceptance will also constrain expansion. A data-center contract must become billable service; software advice must become a deployed change. Someone carries upfront spending and failure costs in both cases. Recurring receipts catching up with construction, or automated review reducing human effort without more incidents, would show those constraints easing. Contract, generation, and addressed counts rising while receipts and delivery lag would warrant narrower expectations for near-term earnings improvement. The technology can still have value while the evidence remains at an earlier commercial stage.

Consistent definitions come first. Google comparisons need fixed output specifications; data centers need consistent billing definitions and cash-flow periods; code review needs comparable repositories, risks, and sample timing. Undisclosed tariffs, depreciation lives, rack costs, and wages cannot be filled with generic industry figures to manufacture precise payback estimates. More cost categories do not ensure completeness either: double-counting customer reimbursements, prepaid construction funding, or asset acquisitions distorts results. Leaving a missing input unresolved is more useful than inventing a unit profit.

Technology buyers need acceptance and usage records. Investors need to connect delivery, cash, and durable recurring revenue. Both need to know who retains the savings. Customers might pay less after a supplier cuts prices, a platform might keep part of the spread, or a team might spend saved credits on more generation. Comparing quoted prices, actual consumption, and final results over the same period shows which changes produce sustainable gains, rather than simply larger activity counts.
