0 · MARKET

Four indices bounce, yields talked back down

Wednesday, September 2. All four major indices closed higher.

IWM bounced hard from the edge of a breakdown, gained 1%, and is holding the 288 to 290 line flagged yesterday, for now.

Nine of eleven S&P sectors rose, so Tuesday's broad selloff did not carry over.

After the close, Broadcom's report whipsawed the stock. Down 6% on the release, up 2% once the call started, then back to roughly down 1% by 8 pm.

The 10-year Treasury yield spiked and reversed. That was the line that mattered today.

Two officials leaned on expectations. Commerce Secretary Lutnick said rates will come down, markets will stabilize, and he is not worried.

Treasury Secretary Bessent said buyback operations are meant to head off adverse market outcomes and free up balance-sheet capacity, so banks can take more of each Treasury auction.

After the jawboning, the 10-year yield did not close at a new high and kept drifting lower into the evening session.

QQQ, SOXX and IWM are all sitting near key levels. A yield that keeps easing adds a layer of support under all three.

Confidence here is medium, not high. The bounce and the yield reversal happened together and point the same way, but one session does not make a trend change.

ADP cooled things slightly. August private payrolls rose 38,000, below July's revised 44,000 and the 47,000 consensus. That is the slowest private hiring since January.

Pay growth for job stayers held at 4.4%. For job changers it slipped from 7.5% to 7.3%.

Manufacturing, professional services and IT were shedding jobs. Education, healthcare, construction and leisure and hospitality kept hiring at a steady pace.

The labor market is cooling, not stalling. Friday's payrolls report gets to confirm or reject that.

1 · COMPUTE

The Beige Book on four fronts: prices, spending, jobs, data centers

The Fed's August Beige Book is its survey of the twelve district banks. It has not been on the radar for a while, and several points this time differ from what the market had assumed.

Prices first. Prices rose in all twelve districts, mostly moderately, sharply in one.

Manufacturers and builders cited input costs pushed up by the Iran war and tariffs. Insurance and healthcare costs added clear pressure.

The sentence that matters is a different one. Consumers have become more price-sensitive, and some firms chose not to pass higher costs through. They accepted lower margins instead.

In plain terms, the pricing lever is close to exhausted. The base is already high. Raise again and the customer walks, so the company eats the cost. Second-half margin assumptions need to move in that direction.

Spending next. Overall healthy, with cracks.

Consumer outlays fell in Cleveland, Richmond and St. Louis, and rose in New York versus July.

Luxury demand stayed strong while everyone else traded down. A mid-priced restaurant reported more traffic because customers cut premium dining. Atlanta and Chicago saw the same value-seeking.

The money is still there. It is just spent with a sharper eye on price.

Jobs. Employment rose in seven districts and was flat in five. Contacts described a low-hire, low-fire market that is tightening overall. That lines up with ADP.

Growth is the heavyweight item. Data centers are driving it.

In Cleveland, labor demand was soft for a fourth straight survey period, yet manufacturing demand boomed on data-center construction. Minneapolis reported strong labor demand for the same reason.

The counterfactual is sharper. St. Louis said construction would slow without data centers. A Chicago contact said construction would be in recession without them.

Tie that to equities. US growth used to run on consumption, roughly 70% to 80% of GDP.

In the past few years the marginal driver has shifted to data centers and AI infrastructure. By a rough estimate, that spending now accounts for close to half of the growth contribution.

That is the real meaning of "AI can only succeed, it cannot fail." It has nothing to do with belief. The economic cost of stopping is unaffordable.

Pull the plug on capex midway and the damage is not confined to semiconductor stocks. Construction, manufacturing, jobs and financial markets take the hit together.

2 · EARNINGS

Broadcom: revenue up, margin down

Broadcom reported fiscal third quarter 2026. Adjusted EPS of $3.32 versus $1.69 a year ago, nearly double, and above the $3.22 Street estimate.

Revenue of $29.6 billion beat the $29.2 billion consensus, up 86% year over year. Adjusted operating margin came in near 68%.

Guidance cut both ways. Fourth quarter revenue is expected to grow 93% to $34.8 billion, slightly above consensus.

Adjusted operating margin guidance of 66% is half a point below expectations and below the third quarter. Margin is slipping quarter by quarter, and that is why the stock fell 6% on the release.

The point made repeatedly here is that Broadcom's forward valuation rests on high growth, and neither leg can slip. Revenue growth on one side, margin on the other. The two together set EPS growth.

The call pulled the stock back. Management now expects AI chip revenue of $115 billion in fiscal 2027 and $230 billion in fiscal 2028, roughly in line with prior expectations and nudged higher.

Net effect: revenue for the year moves up, margin moves down, EPS barely changes. A ten-cent adjustment does not register on a several-hundred-dollar stock.

So the forward valuation range is unchanged at $352 to $369 for the October 2026 fiscal year.

That fiscal year ends shortly, and the market is about to roll to next year's numbers. The next-year range looks deeply undervalued on paper, but it is built on high-growth assumptions with a multiple already above the historical peak range. It is not a margin of safety.

For trading, $325 to $365 is the comfortable band under the high-growth case, with room to push higher while the $200-billion-plus fiscal 2028 revenue expectation keeps advancing.

The level to watch is $324. Lose it and the market is no longer paying the high forward multiple. It has started pricing the slowdown early, and that is not the time to hold and hope.

On the other side, SOXX reached its own key level this week and bounced at support rather than breaking. Broadcom sits in roughly the same spot, so a tactical rebound is on the table.

Further out, going from $100-billion-plus to $200-billion-plus is one thing. Expecting another double to $400 billion in a year is not realistic given the size of the market.

By 2027, when investors look ahead to 2028 capex, slower growth is close to certain. Slower is not negative. It is a base effect. If you can only handle upside in this name, pick a different one.

3 · MARKET

Palo Alto: a strong print cannot outrun slower growth

Palo Alto dropped 9% on Wednesday.

Fiscal fourth quarter 2026 beat comfortably, and the next-quarter guide beat too. The problem is the new fiscal year.

Fiscal 2027 guidance implies revenue growth of 23% to 24%, and next-generation security ARR growth of 22% to 23%. That is a sharp step down from the quarter just reported. Slower is not negative, but the market punishes the slope.

This is the risk flagged earlier on the cybersecurity group. A premium multiple requires growth to stay high, and any deceleration signal in the outlook gets punished.

The logic applies to every stock. Growth is fast off a small base and must slow as the base grows. An investor who only ever chases "it is expensive, but they will raise again" eventually chases straight into the deceleration point.

All three cybersecurity names were judged overvalued here. Once growth slows, the price follows.

By how much? Into the new fiscal year, EPS-growth-based fair value tops out around $164 for the July 2027 fiscal year and $197 for the July 2028 fiscal year.

That growth rate at 40 times is already rich. 50 or 60 times is harder to defend. Even with another 20% raise, $360-plus is very expensive. There is little to argue on fundamentals, which leaves the chart.

The chart is thin. Wednesday closed at a recent low, back inside $317 to $342.

Nearby support is sparse because the run from $276 was fast and lightly traded. The $276 to $317 zone counts as medium support at best, a place to watch for stabilization with no guarantee it holds.

Once a stock has telegraphed slower growth, there is no need to rush in on fundamentals.

Holders have two things to watch. First, the pattern: a two-month top built since the June breakout. Second, the risk: a break of $317 sits close to the late-July low near $310 and would confirm that top. Anyone sitting on a large gain can take it and not be wrong.

4 · LIQUIDITY

Nvidia: the pitch works, volume confirms

Nvidia's gain on Wednesday was pure confidence injection.

Jensen Huang told the G20 technology summit that Nvidia will put nearly $1 trillion into US infrastructure this year alone. He stressed that every country needs infrastructure to support its economy, and urged governments not to let fear of AI dominate the conversation. "The worst outcome is being left behind."

Take it with the appropriate discount. The shovel seller can only build the industry. He cannot stand up and say overbuilding AI will cost jobs. Politically, that is not an option.

But read alongside the Beige Book, the pitch has a real foundation. AI investment is already in a position it cannot back out of. Pull the board midway and who absorbs the consequences?

Power plants, data centers and grid capacity have just been funded. Several states are pushing back against data centers competing with residents for resources. Every participant has to keep pushing until it genuinely cannot continue.

That forced retreat is still a long way off, so everyone keeps adding.

Investors do not need to pick a political side. The only question is what the consequence is for the stock.

Ideology is ideology; investing follows earnings growth. Europe's experiment in screening out polluters to build green ETFs is the proof, judged by years of performance.

Nvidia's earnings growth is real. When the CEO says the spend is this large, the market buys.

Wednesday's volume was the heaviest since early July apart from the August 27 earnings session. The pitch worked, and volume confirmed it.

The Nvidia view is unchanged. Hold, no changes, second-largest position at 10% of the portfolio, constructive above $215. The trade-off is that the stock is too large to be volatile, and what that buys is relative stability.
