1 · MARKET

Starlink bought indoor spectrum. The carriers fell first

SpaceX, S-P-C-X, agreed to buy a nationwide license portfolio from Grain Management, up to fourteen megahertz of paired eight-hundred-megahertz spectrum. The Wall Street Journal put the price at about eight billion dollars in cash. SpaceX already holds higher-frequency licenses near two gigahertz. The low band is meant to reach indoors and push Starlink toward mobile service. The news hit Thursday evening and traded on Friday.

T-Mobile closed at one hundred forty-eight dollars and fifty-eight cents, down thirteen point two seven percent. The session low was one hundred forty-seven dollars and ninety cents. Verizon and A-T-and-T were sold with it. During the session Verizon was pacing its worst day since two thousand two, down about seven percent, and A-T-and-T was down about ten percent. At the open the three carriers had shed about thirty-nine billion dollars of market value, close to five times the price of the spectrum. SpaceX was up only about one percent at midday.

What sold off is the competition, not subscribers who have already left. Eight hundred megahertz travels far and gets through walls. Owning the license is not the same as having the network built. The F-C-C chair said more competition in spectrum is good for consumers, and that more than one hundred billion dollars of spectrum is likely to come to market over the next two years. That is later supply, not Friday's earnings. Without new evidence of lost subscribers, thirteen percent is not share already taken. SpaceX up about one percent does not mean the market dismissed the deal either. The carriers lost several times what the licenses cost.

2 · MARKET

The tower rally is a tenant guess

Crown Castle, C-C-I, closed up eleven point three nine percent at seventy-six dollars and seventy-four cents. American Tower was up nearly seven percent during the session. The story is that indoor low-band coverage could mean a satellite operator eventually rents sites, power, and backhaul. Crown Castle said on its second-quarter call that it would welcome satellite operators. There was no company announcement on Friday, and no new contract. Buying spectrum is not a commitment to build.

A-T-and-T is about twenty-eight percent of Crown Castle's site-rental revenue. If the carriers cut spending because of a new competitor, the towers' biggest customers get weaker too. Carriers down and towers up cannot both be finished facts. The eleven percent is a guess that got bought first. Without a lease, seventy-six seventy-four is not confirmation. The carriers are trading competition. The towers are trading capital spending that has not shown up.

The Philadelphia semiconductor fund, S-O-X-X, closed Friday at five hundred sixty dollars and twenty-six cents, down zero point five four percent. Thursday's close was five hundred sixty-three dollars and twenty-eight cents, down three point three five percent, on about ten point four million shares. On a day the indexes rose, the semiconductor fund did not reclaim. It just softened a little more, on much less volume.

The low is still above the five hundred fifty to five hundred seventy-seven band from Thursday. A break of five hundred fifty only extends the chop down to four hundred eighty-six. The view changes at four hundred eighty-six, about fifteen percent under the close. Friday was not a high-volume break, and it did not change Thursday's structure. While five hundred fifty holds, the extra half point is not an exit. Semiconductors lead again only with volume back through five hundred seventy-seven. A zero point six percent Nasdaq gain does not do that job.
