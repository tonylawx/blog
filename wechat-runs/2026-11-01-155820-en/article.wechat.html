Terra lowers token prices

On October twenty sixth, OpenAI's official developer update log announced G P T six point one Terra. The standard price is one dollar per million input tokens. Cached input costs five cents per million tokens. Output costs five dollars per million tokens.

BleepingComputer reported the launch and pricing the same day. Enterprises now have a publicly available model with clear prices. Those prices tell buyers what model calls cost. They still need to calculate the cost of delivering work that meets their standards.

Cheaper input can lower the cost of repeatedly reading documents, spreadsheets, and interfaces. An agent that works through several steps still pays for reasoning and output. Tools may carry extra charges too. A failed attempt may require reading the context again and repeating the work.

Buyers need to add up every model call through completion. They then need to include human review time. That gives them a basis for comparing the new approach with their existing workflow.

Long documents need particular care. OpenAI's model documentation sets a higher rate when input exceeds two hundred and seventy two thousand tokens. Input and cached input for the entire request then cost twice the standard rates. Output costs one point five times the standard rate.

Writing data into the cache carries separate charges. Five cents per million tokens therefore does not cover every cache operation. The one dollar input rate also does not apply to every long document request. Putting every file into the context may change a bill that initially looked cheap.

Errors are harder to price. A formatting mistake while organizing information might take seconds to fix. In finance or procurement, someone has to find the error, explain it, and correct it. A pilot needs a fixed set of tasks and consistent acceptance criteria. Failed attempts belong in the cost calculation too. Buyers can then measure success without assuming a success rate in advance.

Terra's pricing makes broader trials more affordable. Recurring paid use still depends on whether total cost per accepted task falls. The public prices alone also cannot establish OpenAI's inference profit margin.

Fusion chooses models to control costs

On October twenty eighth, GitHub announced that Copilot Fusion was generally available. Fusion routes tasks across models and coordinates their work. Users can select Standard, High, or Max. Administrators can limit which models are available.

Fusion can use one model for a task. It can escalate a difficult task to another model. It can also have one model draft and another review. Each actual call is still billed at the underlying model's own standard rate.

Independent evaluator Artificial Analysis tested Fusion Max the same day. Fusion ran in the Copilot command line tool. The comparison used Claude Code running Claude Opus five point five Max. The evaluator's Coding Agent Index, version two point zero, weights three component tests equally.

Fusion's average model A P I cost was five dollars and fifty nine cents per benchmark task. The comparison configuration cost eleven dollars and forty two cents. These are costs for the tested tasks. The two systems have different configurations, so the result cannot be credited to one underlying model alone.

Roughly halving the model bill is worth testing further. The measurement is average model A P I cost per benchmark task. It does not establish the cost per successful task. It also does not mean every task receives the same saving.

Subscription fees and human review are excluded. GitHub also offers paid plan users a temporary thirty percent discount on model charges through November thirtieth. Buyers cannot apply that discount again to these benchmark figures to claim a lower long term cost.

Routing works economically when the system judges task difficulty correctly. Simple steps may need fewer expensive model calls. Misrouting, extra reviews, and repeated escalation can add those costs back.

For GitHub parent Microsoft, the useful question is whether developers keep trusting the system to choose models for them. Enterprise buyers need to compare acceptance rates and rework within the same codebase. Review hours, rollback rates, and delays on the slowest tasks matter too. A smaller model bill may deliver little project benefit if rework increases.

Helios enters testing at Meta

On October twenty seventh, A M D disclosed that the first Helios systems had been installed at Meta. Meta was testing them. TechTarget reported on that announced status on October twenty ninth. Its independent industry reporting noted that a date for broader deployment had not been disclosed.

A M D and Meta have reached testing with real systems. This does not establish that production service at scale has begun. TechTarget's coverage also does not amount to an independent test of system performance.

Once the racks are installed, the customer still has to run its business workloads on them. Networking and cooling affect stability. Power and facilities need to be ready too. Software must be adapted, and systems must recover from failures.

Industry analysts interviewed by TechTarget emphasized these delivery requirements. A single chip's advertised capability cannot establish how much usable computing capacity a group of racks provides. That depends on the customer's actual workloads.

A second supplier's value has to account for migration and ongoing maintenance. Every model or software update may need extensive custom tuning. In that case, engineering hours can consume the procurement savings. Adaptation work that can be reused makes a new platform easier to fund through regular budgets.

Neither A M D's announcement nor TechTarget's report provided comparable Helios task cost or energy use data. They therefore do not establish that A M D delivers cheaper training or inference.

For A M D, moving from testing to deliveries the customer accepts would help turn partnership expectations into business. For Meta, another hardware option that runs reliably could strengthen its bargaining position. That could matter when buying capacity and expanding. Installation and testing remain an intermediate step.

Over the next two to four quarters, watch for disclosed production workloads and service milestones. Compare that progress with delivery related revenue. Check actual utilization too, if comparable disclosures become available. A lack of public detail leaves the outcome uncertain. It does not establish that testing has failed.

Measure accepted work over the next two to four quarters

Over the next two to four quarters, enterprises have more reason to judge A I spending by the accepted work it delivers. Terra lowers some model call rates. Fusion tries to reduce expensive model usage. Helios adds a hardware option. Each addresses one part of the cost chain. These developments have yet to establish lower costs for the finished results business users receive.

For software, use the same real tasks and the same quality threshold. Count model and tool charges across every retry. Then add human review costs. This shows whether lower model prices reduce total cost. Terra may need frequent escalation to pricier models. Fusion may require more rework after its promotion ends. Either outcome would weaken the case that low prices can sustain recurring paid use.

Stable acceptance rates would strengthen that case. So would less human intervention. Savings also need to persist after promotions end. Together, those results would provide stronger economic support for routing and model substitution. For hardware, watch when test systems begin handling stable production workloads. Check whether deliveries generate revenue. Customers can gain procurement options while remaining constrained by software adaptation and facility readiness.

Evidence of sustained operation and expansion would support greater confidence in revenue from alternative platforms. One installation announcement is insufficient grounds to include the full potential partnership scale in near term earnings expectations. First ask how much work meets the customer's standards for the same budget. Then ask why the supplier can retain that spending. Low token prices make experiments cheaper. Profits still depend on repeatable delivery.

In the next round of competition, will customers pay for cheap calls, or for results that require less rework?
